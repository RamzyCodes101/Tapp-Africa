Why we built TAPP

The psychology of a tap.

Six ideas from behavioral science explain why paper business cards fail — and why a single tap works better. Scroll to see them unfold.

Every relationship starts with a tiny, forgettable moment.
This is the science of making it stick.

Beat 01 — The Forgetting Curve

You'll forget most of today by tomorrow.

Psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus found we lose over half of new information within an hour, and nearly 70% within a day — including the person you just met.

A business card sitting in a drawer doesn't fight that curve. It just waits to be thrown away.

Beat 02 — Loss Aversion

Paper doesn't survive contact with real life.

It gets left on a table, soaked in a bag, tossed by accident. Behavioral economists have shown the sting of losing something outweighs the pleasure of gaining it — so every lost card is a small, real loss.

TAPP can't be lost in a drawer. Your profile lives online, not in a pocket.

Beat 03 — The Primacy Effect

You decide who someone is in seconds.

Solomon Asch's classic research showed the first few things we learn about a person color everything that follows. A fumbled card exchange is a bad first impression — a confident tap is a good one.

One motion. No fumbling for a pen, an app, or a spelling of your name.

Beat 04 — Reciprocity

Make it easy to give, and people give back.

Robert Cialdini's research on reciprocity found people feel a pull to return a favor — even a small one, like being handed everything they need without having to ask twice.

TAPP hands over your number, socials, portfolio and links upfront. No "can I get your Instagram?"

Beat 05 — Staying Current

Most cards are outdated the day you print them.

New job, new number, new handle — a paper card can't keep up, so it quietly becomes wrong. That small mismatch erodes trust every time it's noticed.

Every TAPP card updates the instant you do. What they tap today is always current.

Beat 06 — The Peak-End Rule

People remember the peak, and the end.

Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman found we judge experiences by their most intense moment and how they finish — not the average. A tap is a small, tactile, slightly surprising peak in an otherwise forgettable exchange.

That's the moment TAPP is built for.
